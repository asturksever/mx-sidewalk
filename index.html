    Mexico counted its sidewalks. Almost nobody has drawn them.

    For its 2020 housing inventory, INEGI recorded for every side of every urban block whether there is a sidewalk: 7.14 million block sides in all 32 states. OpenStreetMap, the open map behind many apps, has drawn a small fraction of that.

    • INEGI has the coverage. 3.71 million block sides with a sidewalk, about 311,000 km.
    • OpenStreetMap has the geometry. Where someone has drawn a sidewalk it sits where the sidewalk is. INEGI’s line follows the edge of the block.
    • They barely overlap. Only 1% of what INEGI counted has also been drawn in OpenStreetMap.
    • Absent is recorded, not guessed. INEGI also says where there is no sidewalk, so a gap on this map is a census finding, not a hole in the data.

    So this map merges them: every sidewalk appears once and keeps a record of where it came from.

    Nearly half of Mexico’s urban block sides have no sidewalk

    INEGI found 3.43 million block sides with no sidewalk: 48% of those it could assess, about 288,000 km of street edge. They are on the map in pink.

    The share varies widely: from 18% of block sides in Aguascalientes to 82% in Oaxaca.

        How three sources become one

        The same method as Sidewalks of the United States and Sidewalks of Canada, with INEGI as the official source. Every line keeps a record of where it came from.

        1. Collect

          INEGI’s Inventario Nacional de Viviendas 2020 block fronts for all 32 states, and every pedestrian way in OpenStreetMap’s Mexico extract: sidewalks, footways, paths, crossings and steps.

        2. Read the road tags

          Where a road carries sidewalk=both, left or right and nobody has drawn the sidewalk, a line is placed beside the road at an estimated offset and shown dashed.

        3. Cut and compare

          Every line is cut into 25 m pieces. A piece matches when a line from the other source lies within 10 to 12 m along most of its length, heading the same way.

        4. Keep each sidewalk once

          A drawn OpenStreetMap sidewalk wins where both have it, because it sits where the sidewalk is. INEGI fills the rest, and a road-tag line survives only where neither has one.

        Read the amber with care. INEGI’s line runs along the block edge, not the sidewalk itself, and records what an enumerator saw in 2020: it does not say how wide the sidewalk is, what state it is in or whether it runs the whole length of the block.
